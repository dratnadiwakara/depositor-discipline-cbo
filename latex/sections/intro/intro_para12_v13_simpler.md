# Version 13: Simpler, more modest framing
*Based on v12. Toned down grand claims ("first causally identified test"); plainer language throughout.*

---

A central insight of banking theory holds that the disciplinary force of deposit contracts derives from their fragility. Calomiris and Kahn (1991) formalize how the sequential service rule embedded in demand deposits disciplines bank managers: the credible threat of early withdrawal by informed, uninsured creditors constrains risk-taking that equity holders and supervisors cannot easily observe. Diamond and Rajan (2001) extend this logic, showing that the vulnerability of demand deposits to runs is inseparable from their governance function. Despite this theoretical consensus, the empirical record has struggled to establish that depositor discipline operates as a causal mechanism rather than an equilibrium correlation. A substantial body of evidence documents that banks with weaker fundamentals pay higher deposit rates and experience faster outflows of uninsured balances (Park, 1995; Martinez Peria and Schmukler, 2001; Maechler and McDill, 2006; Egan, Hortaçsu, and Matvos, 2017), and Iyer, Puri, and Ryan (2016) provide micro-level evidence, using data from an Indian bank, that uninsured depositors respond to genuine solvency risk while insured depositors remain largely unresponsive. But much of this evidence is correlational or drawn from crisis periods in which government interventions — blanket guarantees, emergency liquidity programs, systemic risk determinations — directly suppressed the mechanism being studied (Bennett et al., 2015; Berger and Turk-Ariss, 2015). The March 2023 bank failures have sharpened the policy stakes: SVB's collapse and the FDIC's subsequent invocation of the systemic risk exception have renewed debate — most recently reflected in the reintroduction of the Main Street Depositor Protection Act in March 2026 — over whether uninsured depositor discipline is a real governance mechanism or a run risk that should be neutralized by broader insurance. We test this question at U.S. community banks, where the conditions for discipline are most favorable: institutions below $10 billion in assets carry no credible government backstop, so uninsured depositors face genuine loss exposure (Flannery and Sorescu, 1996; O'Hara and Shaw, 1990).

Isolating a causal depositor discipline effect requires confronting an endogeneity problem that the prior literature has not fully resolved. The most basic challenge is simultaneous determination: bank risk, deposit composition, and depositor behavior co-evolve, so cross-sectional regressions of deposit flows on risk measures capture an equilibrium outcome rather than a causal response; Covitz et al. (2004) show that prior tests conflate depositor-driven reactions with non-discipline factors such as institutional portfolio rebalancing. A second problem is that standard risk measures are themselves contaminated by accounting discretion: under the incurred-loss model (ILM), banks could defer credit loss recognition in ways that obscured credit risk from outside monitors, and Bushman and Williams (2012, 2015) document that this delay weakened external discipline by reducing the informativeness of reported figures. Third, crisis-period samples introduce a further confound, since the episodes with the greatest cross-bank variation in distress are also the episodes in which government guarantees most strongly suppress the discipline channel (Bennett et al., 2015). We address all three problems by exploiting the mandatory adoption of the CECL accounting standard for community banks in 2023Q1. The Day-One retained earnings adjustment required at adoption quantifies lifetime expected credit losses on the existing loan portfolio — losses deferred under the ILM that were mechanically determined by loan vintages assembled years before adoption. Because the adjustment cannot be influenced by contemporaneous managerial decisions or depositor behavior, it provides cross-bank variation in disclosed credit risk that is plausibly orthogonal to current funding conditions. Kim et al. (2026) and Gee et al. (2022) confirm that CECL disclosures are genuinely informative — predicting subsequent realized charge-offs and improving analyst forecast accuracy — while Nier and Baumann (2006) and Chen et al. (2022) show that improvements in disclosure quality of precisely this type are the channel through which depositor discipline is activated. The 2023Q1 community bank setting provides a clean backdrop: no pandemic-era liquidity distortions, no zero lower bound, and no blanket government backstops that would suppress the response we seek to measure.
