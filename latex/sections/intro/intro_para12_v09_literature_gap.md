# Version 9: Empirical Gap / Critique of Prior Literature Hook
*Angle: Acknowledge the literature, show what it cannot establish, motivate the need for identification, position community banks as the under-studied and most policy-relevant population*

---

A substantial empirical literature documents that uninsured depositors respond to bank risk. Studies find that banks with weaker fundamentals pay higher deposit rates, face faster outflows of uninsured balances, and — in some specifications — experience faster earnings reversals when exposed to adverse signals. Yet despite the volume of this evidence, the causal interpretation remains elusive. The identification challenge is fundamental: bank risk measures, deposit composition, and depositor behavior are jointly determined by the same underlying characteristics — the bank's business model, its competitive position, and the clientele it attracts. Riskier banks may pay higher rates, but they may also attract depositors who are themselves less sensitive to risk, or who have concentrated relationships that make switching costly. What looks like discipline in the data may be equilibrium pricing in a segmented deposit market. The most credible identification strategies exploit banking crises for variation in bank-specific distress, but crisis episodes are precisely the periods in which government interventions — emergency guarantees, systemic risk determinations, extraordinary liquidity programs — attenuate and distort the deposit discipline channel. The result is a literature rich in correlational evidence but thin on clean causal estimates, a gap whose policy stakes have grown since March 2023. The failure of Silicon Valley Bank and the subsequent introduction of the Main Street Depositor Protection Act — bipartisan legislation proposing to extend FDIC coverage to $5 million per non-interest-bearing account — have placed the question of whether depositor discipline is real directly at the center of banking policy. If the mechanism is meaningful, the proposed legislation would destroy it. If it is an artifact of endogenous bank–depositor sorting, the objection collapses. Evidence from community banks — the 4,400 institutions with assets below $10 billion that are the legislation's explicit focus and that operate without implicit government guarantees — is what is missing and what we provide.

Generating that evidence requires an information shock that satisfies two conditions: its content must be determined before depositors can condition their behavior on it, and it must be revealed in a non-crisis environment free of confounding policy responses. The mandatory adoption of the CECL accounting standard for community banks in the first quarter of 2023 satisfies both. At adoption, each bank was required to record a Day-One adjustment to retained earnings reflecting the lifetime expected credit losses on its existing loan portfolio — losses deferred under the prior incurred-loss model. The magnitude of this adjustment is a mechanical function of the historical composition of loan vintages: the vintage mix of the loan book, the product of lending decisions made under a prior regime in which managers had no incentive to price in future CECL consequences. This predetermination is the key identification feature: the cross-bank variation in disclosed risk is determined by the historical portfolio, not by current managerial choices or current funding conditions. Banks with large adjustments did not choose to disclose more risk in 2023; they disclosed the risk that their historical lending had already locked in. Community banks in 2023Q1 offer the additional advantage of a clean macroeconomic environment — no zero lower bound, no pandemic-era liquidity inflows, no unprecedented fiscal transfers — and no credible too-big-to-fail guarantee to attenuate the depositor response. The 2020 large-bank adoption window, by contrast, is contaminated by precisely these confounds, which is why community banks are not merely a convenient sample but the necessary one.
