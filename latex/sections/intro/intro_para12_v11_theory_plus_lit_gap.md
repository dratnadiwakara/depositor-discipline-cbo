# Version 11: Theory-First + Literature Gap Hybrid
*Angle: Clean theoretical foundation → what the prior literature has and has not established → why 2023 made it urgent → community banks as the right lab → full identification critique → CECL as solution*
*Citations: from papers_catalog_extended_20260401.md*

---

A central insight of banking theory holds that the disciplinary force of deposit contracts derives from their fragility. Calomiris and Kahn (1991) formalize how the sequential service rule embedded in demand deposits disciplines bank managers: the credible threat of early withdrawal by informed, uninsured creditors constrains risk-taking that equity holders and supervisors cannot easily observe. Diamond and Rajan (1999) extend this logic, showing that the very vulnerability of demand deposits to runs — often viewed as a systemic liability — is inseparable from their governance function. Yet despite the theoretical consensus, the empirical record has struggled to establish that depositor discipline operates as a causal mechanism rather than as an equilibrium correlation. A substantial body of evidence documents that banks with weaker fundamentals pay higher deposit rates and experience faster outflows of uninsured balances (Park, 1995; Martinez Peria and Schmukler, 2001; Maechler and McDill, 2006; Egan, Hortaçsu, and Matvos, 2017), and Iyer, Puri, and Ryan (2016) provide compelling episode-level evidence that uninsured depositors fled Washington Mutual as solvency risk mounted. But this evidence is largely correlational or drawn from crisis periods in which government interventions — blanket guarantees, emergency liquidity programs, systemic risk determinations — directly suppressed the mechanism being studied (Bennett and Hwa, 2015; Berger and Turk-Ariss, 2015). The March 2023 bank failures sharpened the stakes: SVB's $42 billion single-day deposit run and the FDIC's subsequent invocation of the systemic risk exception to guarantee all uninsured deposits have reinvigorated a policy debate — most recently crystallized by the reintroduction of the Main Street Depositor Protection Act in March 2026 — over whether depositor discipline is a real governance mechanism worth preserving or a run channel that should be neutralized by broader insurance. This paper provides the first causally identified test of depositor discipline in a non-crisis, non-TBTF setting — at U.S. community banks, institutions below $10 billion in assets where Flannery and Sorescu's (1996) insight applies most directly: discipline operates precisely when implicit government guarantees are absent.

Identifying a causal depositor discipline effect requires solving an endogeneity problem that is deeper than it first appears, and one that the prior literature has not fully addressed. The most basic challenge is simultaneous determination: bank risk measures, deposit composition, and depositor behavior co-evolve — a bank's loan portfolio characteristics, deposit pricing, and depositor clientele all reflect the same underlying business model and competitive environment. Cross-sectional regressions of deposit flows on risk measures therefore capture an equilibrium outcome, not a causal channel; Covitz and Hancock (2004) make this point explicitly, showing that prior tests conflate depositor-driven responses with non-discipline factors such as institutional portfolio rebalancing. A second, subtler problem is that the risk measures themselves are contaminated by accounting discretion. Under the incurred-loss model (ILM), banks could defer credit loss recognition — a form of earnings management that systematically obscured credit risk from outside monitors; Bushman and Williams (2012, 2015) show that this delay weakened external discipline precisely by reducing the informativeness of accounting disclosures. Crisis-period identification, finally, introduces a third confound: the episodes that generate the most variation in bank-specific distress are also the episodes in which government interventions and blanket guarantees suppress the depositor discipline channel (Bennett and Hwa, 2015), so estimated effects will be attenuated toward zero in exactly the samples where variation in perceived risk is highest. We overcome all three problems by exploiting the mandatory adoption of the CECL accounting standard for community banks in 2023Q1 as a predetermined information shock. The Day-One retained earnings adjustment required at adoption quantifies lifetime expected credit losses on the existing loan portfolio — losses deferred under the ILM and mechanically determined by the historical composition of loan vintages assembled years before adoption. This predetermination is the paper's key identification asset: because the adjustment cannot be influenced by contemporaneous managerial choice or depositor behavior, it provides cross-bank variation in disclosed credit risk that is plausibly orthogonal to current funding conditions. Kim and Kim (2023) and Gee and Neilson (2022) confirm that CECL disclosures are genuinely informative — improving analyst forecast accuracy and predicting subsequent realized charge-offs — while Nier and Baumann (2006) and Chen and Goldstein (2022) establish that the type of disclosure improvement CECL represents is precisely the channel through which depositor discipline is activated. Community banks in 2023Q1 provide the necessary complement: no pandemic-era liquidity flood, no zero lower bound, no blanket government backstops — the clean macroeconomic and institutional environment in which the mechanism, if it exists, can be detected.
