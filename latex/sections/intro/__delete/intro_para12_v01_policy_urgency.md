# Version 1: Policy Urgency Hook
*Angle: SVB → FDIC emergency guarantee → insurance expansion debate → community banks as the stakes*

---

On March 10, 2023, Silicon Valley Bank failed after $42 billion in deposits — overwhelmingly uninsured — left the institution in a single day. Two days later, the Treasury Secretary, the FDIC, and the Federal Reserve invoked the systemic risk exception to guarantee all deposits at SVB and Signature Bank, extending de facto federal protection far beyond the statutory $250,000 ceiling. The episode did not settle the underlying question; it sharpened it. In November 2025, Senators Hagerty and Alsobrooks introduced the Main Street Depositor Protection Act — a bipartisan bill that would raise FDIC coverage up to $5 million per account for non-interest-bearing transaction accounts, re-introduced in March 2026 with expanded cosponsorship. The bill's primary rationale is protection of the businesses, municipalities, and nonprofits that are the principal depositors of the nation's 4,400 community banks — institutions with assets under $10 billion that fund local economies far from Wall Street. Whether that protection is warranted depends on a prior empirical question: do uninsured depositors at community banks actually discipline risk? If they do, eliminating their exposure would dismantle a governance mechanism that constrains moral hazard in the institutions that most directly finance the real economy. If they do not, the case for expanded coverage is straightforwardly protective. We provide the first causally identified answer to this question.

Answering it requires solving an identification problem that the existing literature has not fully resolved. Bank risk exposure, funding structure, and deposit composition are jointly determined: riskier institutions tend to attract rate-sensitive depositors, price their liabilities to reflect perceived risk, and may proactively restructure balance sheets in anticipation of depositor responses. Cross-sectional variation in risk measures and deposit flows therefore reflects a mixture of causal discipline, selection into bank–depositor matches, and endogenous bank behavior. The alternative — exploiting crises for variation in bank-level risk — introduces the opposite problem: the episodes that generate the most variation in perceived distress are precisely the episodes in which government interventions, blanket guarantees, and extraordinary liquidity programs actively distort the mechanism being measured. We exploit the mandatory adoption of the Current Expected Credit Losses (CECL) accounting standard for community banks in the first quarter of 2023 as a predetermined information shock. At adoption, each bank recorded a one-time Day-One adjustment to retained earnings equal to the lifetime expected losses on its existing loan portfolio — losses that had been systematically deferred under the prior incurred-loss regime. Because the magnitude of this adjustment is determined by the historical composition of loan vintages assembled years before adoption, it is not subject to contemporaneous managerial discretion, does not coincide with a systemic crisis, and generates cross-bank variation in disclosed credit risk that is plausibly orthogonal to current funding conditions. The community bank setting is ideal: it is clean of the pandemic-era confounds that compromise the 2020 large-bank adoption window, and the absence of credible implicit guarantees ensures that any discipline we detect reflects genuine market monitoring — not the shadow of a bailout backstop.
