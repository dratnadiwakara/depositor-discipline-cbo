# Version 8: Bank Opacity / Information Asymmetry Hook
*Angle: Bank loan books are opaque → CECL as transparency shock → why community banks are where discipline should be hardest and most interesting to find*

---

Bank loan books are opaque. Unlike traded securities that carry continuous market valuations, traditional loan portfolios are carried at historical cost, leaving creditors with limited real-time information about the credit quality of the assets against which their deposits are claims. This opacity is not incidental — it is the defining feature that makes bank governance hard. Uninsured depositors who bear first losses above the deposit insurance threshold are theoretically the creditors with the sharpest monitoring incentives, but they cannot easily observe whether the bank's loan book is sound or whether losses have been quietly accumulating under accounting rules that allow banks to defer recognition until a loss has been incurred. The Current Expected Credit Losses (CECL) accounting standard, adopted by U.S. community banks in the first quarter of 2023, was designed precisely to pierce this opacity. By requiring banks to recognize lifetime expected credit losses on their existing portfolios at adoption, CECL forced a one-time, non-discretionary reckoning with deferred credit risk. At the moment of adoption, the Day-One adjustment to retained earnings provided depositors, analysts, and counterparties with a bank-specific, quantified signal of how much credit risk had been embedded in the existing loan book — risk that the prior incurred-loss regime had systematically concealed. This paper asks whether community bank depositors used that signal. The answer bears directly on a policy debate that has intensified since 2023: if uninsured depositors at community banks respond to credible credit risk disclosures, then expanding deposit insurance — as proposed in the Main Street Depositor Protection Act of 2025–2026 — would eliminate a monitoring mechanism that supplements supervisory oversight at the 4,400 institutions that most directly fund the real economy.

Turning this research design into causal evidence requires navigating an endogeneity problem that runs deep in the banking literature. Bank risk, deposit structure, and depositor behavior are simultaneously determined: a bank with a high-risk loan portfolio may offer higher deposit rates, attract rate-sensitive uninsured depositors, and manage its liability structure in anticipation of monitoring pressure — all of which creates correlations between risk and deposit outcomes that do not reflect causal discipline. The bank's own accounting choices compound this: under the prior incurred-loss model, banks could manage the timing and extent of loss recognition, meaning that a bank's reported risk measures at any given point reflected both underlying asset quality and managerial discretion over provisioning. Crisis-period evidence addresses selection bias to a degree but introduces its own distortions: the events that generate the most variation in perceived bank distress simultaneously generate the government interventions, emergency guarantees, and macroeconomic confounds that mask the discipline channel. The CECL Day-One adjustment solves the endogeneity problem because it is not a managerial choice: it is a mechanical accounting consequence of the historical composition of the loan book, determined by lending decisions made years before adoption under a regime in which expected-loss accounting was not yet required. Its magnitude is therefore plausibly exogenous to current depositor behavior — a predetermined signal of credit quality, revealed at a regulatory deadline in 2023Q1, in a macroeconomic environment uncontaminated by the pandemic-era liquidity flood that compromised the parallel large-bank adoption in 2020. Community banks are not just a convenient sample; they are the right population — the one where depositor discipline should be most directly measurable, and most consequential for policy.
