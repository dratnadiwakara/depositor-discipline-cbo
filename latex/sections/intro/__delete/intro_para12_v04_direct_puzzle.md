# Version 4: Direct Puzzle Hook
*Angle: Open with the unresolved empirical question directly, then show why 2023 made it urgent, then the challenge*

---

Do uninsured depositors discipline community banks? The question sounds settled — a generation of theoretical work formalized why they should — but credible causal evidence that they do has proved elusive. The March 2023 banking turmoil made the gap in the empirical record impossible to ignore. Silicon Valley Bank suffered a run of $42 billion in uninsured deposits in a single trading session; Signature Bank followed two days later. Regulators invoked an emergency systemic risk exception to guarantee all deposits at both institutions — a backstop that extended de facto federal insurance far beyond the $250,000 statutory ceiling. The episode illustrated the discipline mechanism operating at speed, then immediately short-circuited it. It also triggered a policy debate that remains unresolved: in November 2025, the Senate introduced the Main Street Depositor Protection Act to raise FDIC coverage to up to $5 million for non-interest-bearing transaction accounts, with the explicit goal of protecting the businesses and municipalities that are the principal uninsured depositors of community banks. Whether that policy change would eliminate a functioning governance mechanism or merely remove a run channel with little disciplinary content depends on an empirical answer the literature has not yet provided. We provide it. Community banks — 4,400 institutions below $10 billion in assets, operating without credible implicit government guarantees — are the right setting: it is precisely here that depositor discipline should be most visible and most policy-relevant, yet prior identification strategies have left this population understudied.

Identifying the causal effect of disclosed credit risk on depositor behavior requires untangling a web of simultaneity. A bank's risk profile, its deposit pricing, and its depositor composition are determined together: aggressively lending institutions attract certain depositor types, compensate for perceived risk through higher rates, and may preemptively restructure their liability base in anticipation of outflows. Cross-sectional regressions of deposit flows on risk measures thus capture equilibrium sorting and endogenous bank responses alongside any causal discipline effect. The natural alternative — exploiting banking crises for variation in bank-level distress — introduces its own confounds: the episodes that generate the most variation in perceived bank risk are also the episodes that generate emergency guarantees, extraordinary liquidity programs, and systemic policy interventions that offset, distort, or mask the depositor response. What is needed is an exogenous, bank-specific revelation of credit risk in a non-crisis environment. We exploit the mandatory adoption of the CECL accounting standard for community banks in the first quarter of 2023. At adoption, each bank recorded a one-time Day-One adjustment to retained earnings quantifying the lifetime expected losses embedded in its existing loan portfolio — losses that the prior incurred-loss model had systematically deferred. The magnitude of this adjustment is mechanically determined by the historical composition of the loan book, assembled before CECL adoption was imminent: it is a disclosure of past credit decisions, not a contemporaneous signal of current management choices. The 2023Q1 community bank setting is clean: no pandemic, no zero interest-rate policy, no unprecedented fiscal intervention — and no TBTF backstop that could attenuate the depositor response we are trying to measure.
