# Version 6: Market Discipline Architecture / Regulatory Design Hook
*Angle: Market discipline as a pillar of regulatory design → where the evidence fails → SVB as pressure test → community banks*

---

Market discipline — the constraint that informed private creditors impose on bank risk-taking through withdrawal and repricing — is a pillar of modern prudential regulation. Basel II institutionalized this logic through Pillar 3, which requires banks to disclose risk exposures sufficient to activate monitoring by shareholders, counterparties, and depositors. The underlying assumption is that uninsured creditors, because they bear first losses above the deposit insurance threshold, have both the incentive and the mechanism to discipline excessive risk-taking when disclosure is adequate. Yet the empirical support for this assumption in U.S. banking has remained surprisingly fragile, and the March 2023 banking turmoil exposed its limits in real time. Silicon Valley Bank failed not because depositors were uninformed, but because they were informed — and when they acted on that information collectively, the run was immediate and total. Regulators responded with an emergency guarantee that, in effect, suspended the discipline mechanism they had long relied upon. This sequence raised a pointed policy question that the Senate's Main Street Depositor Protection Act — introduced in November 2025 and reintroduced in March 2026 — places at the center of legislative debate: is depositor discipline at U.S. community banks a meaningful governance mechanism worth preserving, or a source of run risk with limited disciplinary content that expanded insurance should neutralize? Answering this question requires evidence from community banks specifically — the 4,400 institutions below $10 billion in assets where TBTF guarantees are absent, the depositor base consists of businesses and local governments with genuine credit risk exposure, and the mechanism should be most clearly operative.

Generating that evidence requires solving an identification problem that the prior literature has skirted. Bank risk measures — nonperforming loan ratios, capital cushions, loss provisions — are themselves endogenous to the deposit market: institutions with fragile funding structures manage capital ratios strategically, provision losses with discretion, and price deposits to retain uninsured balances. The resulting simultaneity means that cross-sectional and panel estimates of the deposit-discipline relationship capture equilibrium bank-depositor sorting alongside any causal channel. Crisis-period identification addresses selection bias partly but generates a distinct confound: the events that create the sharpest variation in perceived bank distress — systemic runs, regulatory interventions, emergency guarantee announcements — simultaneously activate government backstops that suppress the discipline mechanism. What the identification strategy requires is a source of cross-bank variation in disclosed credit risk that is determined by historical, pre-adoption decisions, revealed in a non-crisis environment, and uncontaminated by contemporaneous managerial discretion. We construct this by exploiting the mandatory adoption of CECL for community banks in 2023Q1. The Day-One retained earnings adjustment required at adoption quantifies lifetime expected credit losses on the existing loan portfolio, determined mechanically by the vintage composition of loans assembled under the prior incurred-loss model — years before adoption. This adjustment is not a managerial choice: it is an accounting consequence of past lending decisions, disclosed at a regulatory deadline, with no accompanying systemic event and no government backstop to attenuate depositor responses. Community banks in 2023Q1 provide the cleanest identification environment available for testing depositor discipline in modern U.S. banking.
