# Version 5: Insurance Reform Bill Hook
*Angle: Lead with the Main Street Depositor Protection Act as the live policy moment, work backward to the empirical question*

---

In March 2026, the United States Senate reintroduced the Main Street Depositor Protection Act, bipartisan legislation that would raise FDIC deposit insurance coverage to between $250,000 and $5 million per account for non-interest-bearing transaction accounts. The bill targets a specific depositor population: the businesses, municipalities, nonprofits, and local governments that hold large transaction balances at community banks and cannot realistically spread deposits across hundreds of institutions to remain within insurance limits. Its sponsors cite the March 2023 failures of Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, and First Republic — and the ad hoc government guarantees that followed — as evidence that the existing $250,000 ceiling leaves a critical segment of the economy unprotected. Opponents warn of a different risk: deposit insurance expansion would eliminate depositor discipline, the mechanism by which uninsured creditors constrain bank moral hazard through monitoring, withdrawal, and repricing. This disagreement is not resolved by theory alone. Depositor discipline is a theoretical prediction with a contested empirical record, and the population at the center of the proposed policy change — uninsured depositors of community banks — is precisely the population for which clean causal evidence is most scarce. This paper fills that gap. We provide the first causally identified test of depositor discipline at U.S. community banks, using a novel information shock that is determined before depositors can respond and is revealed in a non-crisis environment free of the government interventions that contaminate prior evidence.

The identification challenge is familiar to the banking literature but has never been satisfactorily resolved for the community bank setting. Risk exposure, liability structure, and depositor behavior are simultaneously determined: a bank that concentrates lending in higher-yield, higher-risk segments attracts depositors who are willing to accept those banks' risk profiles, may price deposits to compensate, and may proactively manage uninsured balances in anticipation of monitoring pressures. The result is that cross-sectional variation in bank risk measures and deposit outcomes reflects an equilibrium — the product of mutual selection and adaptation — rather than a causal discipline channel. Crisis-period identification fares no better in this sector: the 2023 banking turmoil, precisely because it induced emergency government guarantees and an FDIC systemic risk determination, distorted the very mechanism it brought into view. We exploit the mandatory adoption of the Current Expected Credit Losses (CECL) standard for community banks in 2023Q1 as an information shock whose content was determined before depositors could condition their behavior on it. The Day-One retained earnings adjustment recorded at adoption reflects lifetime expected losses on loan portfolios assembled under the prior incurred-loss regime — a mechanical, non-discretionary disclosure of historical credit risk. Its cross-bank variation is driven by legacy loan portfolio characteristics, not by contemporaneous managerial choices or current funding conditions. Community banks are the ideal and necessary laboratory: unlike large banks, whose 2020 CECL adoption overlapped with pandemic-era interventions that preclude causal inference, community banks in 2023Q1 operated in a stable macroeconomic environment without implicit government guarantees — the setting where the discipline mechanism, if it exists, should be cleanly detectable.
