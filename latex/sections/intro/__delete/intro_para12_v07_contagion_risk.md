# Version 7: Contagion / Run Speed Angle
*Angle: Lead with the speed and scale of the SVB run → tension between discipline and contagion → community banks as the place where this trade-off is resolved*

---

The collapse of Silicon Valley Bank on March 10, 2023 was not a slow deterioration — it was a single-day withdrawal of $42 billion in deposits, with another $100 billion staged to leave the following morning. Ninety-four percent of SVB's deposits were uninsured, and the bank's concentrated uninsured depositor base, once alarmed, acted with a speed that overwhelmed the institution before supervisors could intervene. What followed exposed a central tension in bank regulatory policy. The very feature that makes uninsured deposits theoretically powerful as a governance instrument — the immediacy of exit — is also what makes them dangerous as a systemic liability. Within 48 hours, Signature Bank had been shuttered; within two months, First Republic had failed. Federal regulators invoked the systemic risk exception to guarantee all uninsured deposits at the resolved institutions, implicitly acknowledging that the discipline mechanism had become a contagion mechanism. This tension — governance function versus run risk — is at the heart of the ongoing debate over deposit insurance reform. The Senate's Main Street Depositor Protection Act, introduced in 2025 and reintroduced in 2026, proposes to expand coverage to up to $5 million for non-interest-bearing accounts, targeted explicitly at the businesses and municipalities that are the primary uninsured depositors of the nation's 4,400 community banks. Whether that expansion would sacrifice meaningful discipline or merely eliminate a run channel with little governance content is an empirical question that this paper answers — in the setting where it matters most, and where it can be answered most cleanly.

The identification challenge is that credible causal evidence of depositor discipline is unusually hard to generate in the community bank sector. Risk exposure, deposit structure, and depositor behavior are jointly determined by underlying bank characteristics: a bank with a concentrated, high-risk loan portfolio attracts depositors of a particular type, offers rates that reflect its risk-return profile, and may proactively manage its uninsured deposit base. These simultaneous adaptations contaminate cross-sectional estimates of the deposit–discipline relationship. Banking crisis studies use aggregate shocks to generate variation in bank-specific risk, but these episodes trigger government guarantees and policy interventions that directly attenuate the mechanism. The result is that existing evidence on depositor discipline is either correlational or identified from settings — large bank failures, systemic crises — where the policy environment confounds the private market response. We exploit the mandatory adoption of the CECL accounting standard for community banks in 2023Q1 as a setting that avoids both problems. The Day-One retained earnings adjustment recorded at adoption reflects the lifetime expected losses embedded in each bank's historical loan portfolio — a predetermined, non-discretionary disclosure of credit risk that does not coincide with any systemic event, any government intervention, or any macroeconomic shock that would independently affect deposit demand. Because the adjustment is mechanically determined by lending decisions made years before adoption, high-CECL banks in 2023 are not necessarily riskier today than low-CECL banks; they have simply disclosed that their historical loan books carried more deferred credit risk. This distinction — a revelation of past risk, not a contemporaneous deterioration — is what allows us to isolate the depositor information channel from the confounds that have obscured it in prior work.
