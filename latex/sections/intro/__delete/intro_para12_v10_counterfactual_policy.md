# Version 10: Counterfactual Policy Hook
*Angle: Start from the policy decision — what happens if we expand insurance? — and show that the answer depends entirely on whether discipline exists at community banks*

---

Suppose Congress passes the Main Street Depositor Protection Act. Under the bipartisan legislation introduced in November 2025 and reintroduced in March 2026, FDIC coverage for non-interest-bearing transaction accounts would rise from $250,000 to up to $5 million per depositor. The businesses, municipalities, and nonprofits that currently hold uninsured balances at community banks would receive full protection; the threat of loss that theoretically motivates their monitoring of bank risk would be eliminated. Whether this is a net benefit depends entirely on an empirical question: does the threat of loss actually generate disciplinary behavior that constrains risk-taking at community banks? If it does, the legislation would destroy a private governance mechanism that supplements regulatory oversight at the 4,400 institutions below $10 billion in assets that are the country's dominant lenders to small businesses and local governments. If it does not — if uninsured depositors at community banks are passive, unable to assess bank risk, or resigned to government backstops — the legislation is straightforwardly protective with limited moral hazard cost. The March 2023 banking turmoil sharpened this trade-off but did not resolve it. The failures of Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, and First Republic, and the emergency deposit guarantees that followed, demonstrated that uninsured depositors can move fast enough to destabilize an institution in a single trading session. Whether they move in response to information about bank risk, rather than in response to panic or peer behavior, is a different question — and it is the empirical question on which the policy debate turns. This paper answers it, using a novel identification strategy that produces causal evidence from the community bank sector where it is most needed and most credible.

Identifying whether depositors respond to disclosed credit risk — as opposed to aggregate panic, peer behavior, or interest rate conditions — requires a source of variation in bank-specific risk revelation that is not contaminated by the factors that typically drive depositor behavior in the data. Risk measures derived from bank financial statements are endogenous: nonperforming loan ratios, capital adequacy measures, and loss provisioning histories reflect both underlying credit quality and the bank's accounting discretion, strategic choices about provisioning timing, and competitive responses to deposit market conditions. Regression estimates that use these measures as right-hand-side variables therefore capture an equilibrium in which risk, deposits, and behavior are jointly determined, not a causal discipline channel. Crisis studies exploit aggregate shocks but generate confounds — government guarantees, extraordinary central bank support, blanket insurance expansions — that run in the opposite direction from depositor discipline and are largest precisely when the discipline channel is most active. We resolve this identification problem by exploiting the mandatory 2023Q1 adoption of CECL for community banks. The Day-One retained earnings adjustment is a non-cash, non-discretionary revision to equity that quantifies lifetime expected credit losses on the existing loan portfolio — the product of historical lending decisions made before banks had any reason to strategically position their portfolios for a CECL disclosure. Because the adjustment is determined by the historical vintage composition of the loan book, it provides cross-bank variation in disclosed credit risk that is predetermined with respect to current depositor behavior: high-CECL banks disclosed more past risk, not necessarily more current risk. The 2023Q1 setting is clean — no pandemic, no near-zero rates, no fiscal transfers — and community banks are the right population, because they receive no implicit government guarantees that would blunt the depositor response we seek to measure.
