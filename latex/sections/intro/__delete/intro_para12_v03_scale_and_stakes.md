# Version 3: Scale and Stakes Hook
*Angle: Lead with the $7T magnitude → community banks as holders → governance argument → policy stakes*

---

Nearly half of all U.S. bank deposits — approximately $7 trillion — exceed the federal deposit insurance limit of $250,000. These uninsured balances are held overwhelmingly by businesses, local governments, nonprofits, and high-net-worth households, and they are concentrated at community banks: the roughly 4,400 institutions with assets below $10 billion that finance local economies, agriculture, and small-business lending across the country. Under the canonical frameworks of Calomiris and Kahn (1991) and Diamond and Rajan (2001), these uninsured depositors are not merely risk-bearers — they are monitors. Because they stand first in line to absorb losses above the insurance threshold, they have the incentive to track bank credit quality, and because they hold demand deposits, they have the mechanism to act. In principle, this private monitoring complements public supervision, constraining moral hazard in institutions that are too numerous and too small to receive close regulatory attention. Whether it operates in practice determines the policy stakes of one of the more consequential debates in post-2023 bank regulation. In November 2025, Congress introduced the Main Street Depositor Protection Act to raise FDIC coverage to up to $5 million for non-interest-bearing transaction accounts — explicitly targeting the community bank depositors who are the subject of this paper. If depositor discipline is real and meaningful at these institutions, expanding insurance would eliminate a governance mechanism that restrains excessive credit risk-taking in the segment of banking that matters most for the real economy.

Establishing that the mechanism is real requires overcoming an identification challenge that has frustrated the prior literature. Bank credit risk, deposit pricing, and deposit composition are jointly determined: the same factors that drive risky lending also shape the depositor clientele, the rates offered to retain uninsured balances, and the bank's own strategic management of its liability structure. Cross-sectional estimates that regress deposit outflows on risk measures therefore capture equilibrium sorting and endogenous bank responses alongside any causal discipline channel. Crisis-period studies address selection partially but introduce a different confound: systemic stress generates large variation in bank-specific risk but simultaneously triggers government interventions — emergency guarantees, extraordinary liquidity facilities, blanket insurance expansions — that directly offset the discipline channel being measured. What is needed is a setting in which variation in disclosed credit risk is determined before the depositor can respond and is revealed in a non-crisis environment. We construct this setting using the mandatory adoption of the CECL accounting standard for community banks in 2023Q1. The Day-One retained earnings adjustment each bank recorded at adoption reflects lifetime expected credit losses on its existing loan portfolio — losses deferred under the prior incurred-loss model and mechanically determined by the historical composition of the loan book. Because the size of the adjustment is a function of lending decisions made years before adoption, it is not subject to contemporaneous managerial discretion and is plausibly uncorrelated with current funding conditions. Community banks in 2023 offer the ideal laboratory: no pandemic, no zero lower bound, no blanket government backstops — just information, depositors, and the market mechanism.
