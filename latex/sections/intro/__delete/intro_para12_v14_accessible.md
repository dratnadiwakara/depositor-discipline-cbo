Banks are fragile by design. Because depositors can withdraw their funds on demand, a bank that takes too much risk faces the threat of a run — and that threat is supposed to discipline bank managers. This is the core idea in Calomiris and Kahn (1991) and Diamond and Rajan (2001): the very fragility of demand deposits is what gives them their governance function. Uninsured depositors — those whose balances exceed the FDIC insurance limit — bear real losses if the bank fails, so they have strong incentives to monitor bank risk and withdraw or demand higher rates when they see trouble. Whether this mechanism actually works in practice, however, is much harder to establish. A large empirical literature finds that riskier banks pay more for deposits and lose more uninsured balances (Park, 1995; Martinez Peria and Schmukler, 2001; Maechler and McDill, 2006; Egan, Hortaçsu, and Matvos, 2017; Iyer, Puri, and Ryan, 2016), but these patterns are difficult to interpret causally: banks that take more risk also attract a different mix of depositors, operate in different markets, and face different competitive conditions. The correlation between risk and deposit outflows could reflect depositor discipline, or it could simply reflect the equilibrium that emerges when risky banks and risk-tolerant depositors find each other. The policy question is urgent regardless: the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 and the FDIC's decision to guarantee all uninsured deposits — including balances far above the standard limit — have renewed a live debate, most recently reflected in the reintroduction of the Main Street Depositor Protection Act in March 2026, over whether uninsured depositor discipline is a governance mechanism worth relying on. We study this question at U.S. community banks, where the conditions for discipline are most favorable: banks with assets below $10 billion carry no plausible government backstop, so uninsured depositors face genuine loss exposure and have clear reasons to act on risk information (Flannery and Sorescu, 1996; O'Hara and Shaw, 1990).

Getting clean causal estimates requires solving three compounding identification problems. First, bank risk and depositor behavior are simultaneously determined — a bank's risk profile, deposit pricing, and depositor mix all evolve together, so it is hard to say whether deposit outflows cause banks to reduce risk, or whether deteriorating fundamentals cause both. Covitz et al. (2004) show that many apparent discipline effects in the prior literature partly reflect institutional investors rebalancing portfolios for reasons unrelated to bank-specific risk. Second, the accounting measures typically used to proxy for bank risk were themselves unreliable: under the old incurred-loss model (ILM), banks were only required to recognize loan losses after they had already occurred, and could delay provisioning in ways that kept credit risk off the books. Bushman and Williams (2012, 2015) show that this opacity weakened market discipline — depositors cannot respond to risk they cannot see. Third, most high-variation episodes in bank distress occur during financial crises, but crises are precisely when governments step in with guarantees that mute the depositor response; estimates from those periods will understate the true disciplinary effect (Bennett et al., 2015). We address all three problems through a single design. In 2023Q1, U.S. community banks were required to adopt the CECL accounting standard, which forces banks to recognize the full expected lifetime credit losses on their existing loan portfolios at the moment of adoption. This produced a one-time, public adjustment to each bank's retained earnings — a number that reflects the credit risk embedded in loan vintages originated years earlier, well before the adoption date. Because the size of this adjustment was determined by historical lending decisions rather than current conditions, it provides cross-bank variation in disclosed credit risk that is plausibly unrelated to anything happening in deposit markets at the time. Kim et al. (2026) and Gee et al. (2022) confirm that CECL disclosures are informative: larger Day-One charges predict higher subsequent charge-offs, and analyst forecast accuracy improves after adoption. Nier and Baumann (2006) and Chen et al. (2022) provide the mechanism link: improvements in bank disclosure quality of exactly this type are what enable depositors to act on risk information. The 2023Q1 community bank setting provides a clean backdrop: no pandemic-era deposit glut, no zero lower bound, and no blanket government guarantees that would suppress the response we seek to detect.
