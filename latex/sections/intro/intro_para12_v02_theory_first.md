# Version 2: Theory-First Hook
*Angle: Calomiris–Kahn / Diamond–Rajan → empirical contest → SVB as renewed urgency → community banks as the right lab*

---

A foundational result in banking theory holds that the disciplinary force of deposit contracts derives precisely from their fragility. Calomiris and Kahn (1991) show that short-term demandable debt held by sequentially served creditors constrains managerial risk-taking: the threat of withdrawal is credible because early redemption is rewarded, and the threat is informationally potent because sophisticated creditors monitor. Diamond and Rajan (2001) extend this logic, demonstrating that the vulnerability of demand deposits to runs — often viewed as a systemic liability — is inseparable from their governance function. Under both frameworks, uninsured depositors who bear first losses above the insurance threshold are the private-sector complement to regulatory supervision: they are the creditors with the sharpest incentives to monitor and the clearest mechanism to act. Yet whether this theoretical mechanism operates in practice has remained vigorously disputed. Crisis-period evidence offers mixed signals, and non-crisis evidence is sparse. The March 2023 banking turmoil raised the empirical stakes dramatically. The failure of Silicon Valley Bank — triggered by a run of uninsured depositors and resolved with an emergency government guarantee — reignited a debate that has since produced a concrete legislative proposal: the Main Street Depositor Protection Act, introduced in November 2025 and reintroduced in March 2026, which would raise FDIC coverage to up to $5 million per account. If the discipline mechanism is real, this expansion would eliminate a governance function that theory assigns a central role. Evidence on this question is most cleanly obtained at community banks — institutions below $10 billion in assets where implicit guarantees are absent and the identification environment is uncontaminated.

The empirical challenge is fundamental. Risk exposure and deposit structure are endogenous to the same bank-level characteristics: a bank that pursues high-yield lending attracts a particular depositor clientele, prices deposits to reflect the risk premium, and may preemptively manage its uninsured liability base in anticipation of depositor monitoring. These simultaneous adjustments mean that cross-sectional correlations between risk measures and deposit flows capture an equilibrium outcome, not a causal channel. Studies that exploit banking crises for identification face the converse problem: crises generate the most useful variation in perceived bank distress but simultaneously generate government interventions, blanket guarantees, and aggregate liquidity shocks that attenuate, distort, or obscure the discipline mechanism. What the literature requires is a setting in which the revelation of bank-specific credit risk is plausibly predetermined with respect to current depositor behavior — a true information shock uncorrelated with contemporaneous funding conditions. We construct this setting by exploiting the mandatory 2023Q1 adoption of the CECL standard for community banks. The Day-One retained earnings adjustment required at adoption quantifies the lifetime expected losses embedded in the existing loan portfolio — losses that were deferred under the prior incurred-loss regime and are mechanically determined by the historical composition of loan vintages assembled long before adoption was imminent. The adjustment therefore reveals information about the past without reflecting current managerial choices, without coinciding with a systemic event, and in a setting — community banking in 2023 — uncontaminated by the pandemic-era liquidity flood that invalidates the parallel large-bank adoption window.
