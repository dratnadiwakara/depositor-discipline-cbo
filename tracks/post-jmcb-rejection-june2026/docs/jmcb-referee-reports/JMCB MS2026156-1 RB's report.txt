Referee Report for “Depositor discipline in Community Banking: Evidence from the CECL Information Shock” – May 2026

	Deposit insurance laws are nearly ubiquitous in the modern marketplace. However, coverage is incomplete, leaving some uninsured depositors in most markets. This paper examines whether those uninsured depositors still discipline their banks through withdrawals. The author makes use of a unique accounting change that required all U.S. banks to disclose their loan risk. While banks did not have to report losses until they were realized, the new Current Expected Credit Loss (CECL) adjustment forced banks to report their potential losses from the start of each loan. So with few exceptions, banks were forced to simultaneously report their existing loan risk. The author focuses on the first disclosure by community banks (under $10 million in capital) in the first quarter of 2023. They find that the ratio of uninsured deposits to assets declined for banks with high CECL disclosures, especially those with more sophisticated depositors. High CECL banks also saw their funding costs rise and profit fall in the quarters following the disclosure. 

	The paper has several notable strengths. It is sitting between several prominent literatures including deposit insurance, market discipline, and non-core funding. The CECL setup is also helpful for identification and is being used in a number of recent studies. The data in the paper seem equal to the task and the author has already conducted a number of robustness checks and extensions. That said, I still have a couple of concerns with the analysis.
 
	My primary concern is the overlap between the CECL disclosure period and the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) and other institutions in March 2023. The timing is problematic for identification because the CECL disclosures for commercial banks coincided exactly with the period of widespread depositor concern and substantial uninsured deposit outflows from community banks. Although the paper employs difference-in-differences and triple-difference specifications, the broader panic environment is difficult to separate from the effects of CECL disclosures themselves. Surprisingly, the manuscript only mentions this issue late in the paper (p. 20) but throughout characterizes the discipline as occurring “absent a crisis trigger,” which seems difficult to reconcile with contemporaneous conditions at community banks (e.g., Caglio et al. 2024). The paper attributes the observed changes in uninsured deposits to market discipline, but it needs to more convincingly rule out fear-driven withdrawals associated with the SVB episode.

	While community banks may not have shared SVB’s concentrated uninsured technology-sector depositor base, their depositors were still influenced by the broader panic. Depositors had ignored SVB’s obvious issues for so long that they might have over-reacted to existing issues at other banks. The SVB collapse thus may have generated a structural shift in how uninsured depositors evaluated bank risk characteristics after March 2023, independent of the CECL disclosures themselves. If banks with larger CECL adjustments also differed systematically in characteristics associated with depositor concern (such as uninsured deposit shares, loan loss allowances, or geographic exposure to technology-oriented regions) then the results may partially reflect heterogeneous responses to the panic rather than reactions to CECL-specific information. 

	A related issue is the speed with which depositor responses appear in some specifications. Unless the CECLs were made public for all banks before the end of the quarter, the near-immediate reaction raises questions about whether depositors were instead responding to broader signals about bank risk already circulating during the first quarter of 2023. Because the paper’s interpretation relies on CECL disclosures conveying new information, the author should engage this issue more directly.

	I have two suggestions that will help address these concerns. First, the author can allow the effects of key bank characteristics measured in 2022:Q4 (e.g., uninsured deposit shares, leverage, bank size, loan quality, profitability, and geographic characteristics) to vary flexibly over time by interacting them with the quarter fixed effects. Doing so would help absorb any post-SVB changes in depositor sensitivity to existing information on bank fundamentals. Second, the author can examine banks that filed unusually early or late CECL disclosures. While reporting timing would be endogenous, evidence that depositor responses aligned more closely with disclosure timing rather than the general SVB panic would provide useful supporting evidence for the proposed mechanism.

	My second major concern relates to where the withdrawn deposits ultimately went. The paper interprets the results as uninsured depositors withdrawing funds in response to perceived bank risk, but the subsequent destination of those funds is important for interpretation and identification. If depositors simply moved funds into community banks with lower CECL exposure, the estimated effects would be mechanically amplified and may violate the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption. In addition, this period saw rapid growth in the use of market-based deposit insurance arrangements that split large deposits across multiple institutions to avoid deposit insurance limits. Kim et al. (2024), for example, document a sharp increase in adoption following SVB’s collapse. Not only might high-CECL banks have shifted into this space more quickly (causing a shift from uninsured to insured deposits) but their more financial sophisticated depositors also may have disproportionately shifted into these arrangements after March 2023. Either of these patterns could have produced the observed deposit changes independent of the information content of the CECL disclosures. Given the paper’s focus on community banks with relatively localized footprints, the branch-level data may allow the author to explore these possibilities in greater detail.

Minor Comments:
-Table 2 – The discussion emphasizes the model’s relatively low explanatory power, but the specification omits several potentially important variables, including deposit composition, reliance on other forms of non-core funding, local market characteristics, and trends in performance and loan quality. Although CECL is driven by long-term loan composition, other balance sheet and market factors may be correlated with the CECL and depositor behavior.

- A growing literature (much of which is already cited in the paper) examines how banks adjusted their strategies in response to CECL disclosures. If banks altered their behavior quickly after implementation, the analysis might be picking up depositors responses to those strategic adjustments rather than directly to the disclosures themselves. The author should engage with this literature at least as much as it could be influencing their results.

-I would encourage the author to explore alternative CECL cutoffs beyond the current 10 percent threshold. Variation across thresholds may help clarify the underlying mechanism. If the results reflect depositor responses to new information, then one would not expect a smooth linear relationship; relatively small CECL adjustments had little informational content regardless of direction, whereas larger adjustments mattered considerably more than the linear relationship would imply. Relatedly, it would be useful to note whether the 10% upper and 10% lower tails of the distribution generate symmetric but opposite depositor responses. 

-Figure 1 – There appears to be an outlier that warrants additional discussion.
