\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This paper provides causal evidence that uninsured depositors
discipline community banks.  When mandatory adoption of the Current
Expected Credit Loss (CECL) standard forced more than 4,000
U.S.\ community banks to disclose lifetime expected credit losses on
their existing loan portfolios in 2023, banks with large disclosed
charges lost uninsured time deposits and gained insured time
deposits.  Whether such discipline exists is
a central question in banking.  Theory predicts it should
\citep{calomiris1991role, diamond2001liquidity}, and riskier banks pay
higher deposit rates and experience larger uninsured outflows
\citep{hannan1988bank, park1995market, park1998market,
martinez2001depositors,
maechler2006dynamic, iyer2016tale, egan2017deposit, chen2024liquidity},
but whether these
patterns reflect a causal disciplinary response or equilibrium
correlation remains unsettled.  The question is pressing: the Main
Street Depositor Protection Act (S.~2999, reintroduced March 2026)
would extend coverage to \$10~million per account, and early state
deposit insurance programs that eliminated market discipline enabled
excessive risk-taking \citep{calomiris2019stealing}.  Community banks
are the right laboratory: institutions below \$10~billion in assets
carry no credible government backstop, so the monitoring incentive is
intact \citep{o1990deposit, flannery1996evidence}.  Consistent with
that incentive surviving, small U.S.\ banks are the one group where
depositor discipline held or strengthened through the 2008--2009
crisis rather than eroding under the expanded guarantees
\citep{berger2015depositors}.

Clean identification requires resolving three problems.  Simultaneity:
a bank's risk profile, deposit composition, and depositor behavior
co-evolve, so regressions of flows on risk measures capture equilibrium
sorting rather than isolated disciplinary responses.  Accounting
opacity: under the incurred-loss model, banks could delay recognition
and systematically understate risk in disclosures
\citep{beatty2011delays, bushman2015delayed}.  Aggregate stress: the
episodes with the greatest variation in bank distress are precisely
those in which expanded government guarantees weaken or obscure the
depositor response \citep{berger2015depositors, bennett2015market}.  I address all
three by exploiting mandatory CECL adoption for community banks in 2023
as an information shock whose cross-sectional intensity was fixed
before the event.  CECL requires banks to recognize lifetime expected
credit losses on existing loan portfolios at adoption, producing a
one-time adjustment to retained earnings whose magnitude reflects loan
vintages assembled years earlier.  Banks knew the standard was coming,
but the size of the Day-One charge, the treatment variation I use, was
pinned down by the composition and seasoning of the existing loan book
rather than by contemporaneous deposit-market conditions.
\citet{gee2022decision} validate the treatment variable directly:
Day-One adjustments predict future net charge-offs and are reflected
in equity prices, and the adjustment is \emph{new} information
for smaller banks, whose information environments are
thinnest.  \citet{nier2006market} show that disclosure and uninsured
funding together strengthen the incentive force of market discipline,
and \citet{chen2022bank} show that uninsured deposit flows respond
more sharply to performance at more transparent banks.

The central result, estimated on quarterly data from 2016 to 2025, is
a level difference-in-differences estimate: after the
disclosure, high-CECL banks lost 0.394 percentage points of assets in
uninsured time deposits and gained 0.712 percentage points of insured
time deposits, with continuous-treatment estimates scaling in
proportion to the disclosed charge.  Event studies over a five-year
pre-period show flat pre-adoption trajectories for the uninsured
series and a divergence that emerged at adoption and persisted.  As a
very conservative robustness check, a triple-difference specification
with bank-by-quarter fixed effects, which compares uninsured to
insured time deposits within the same bank and quarter and absorbs
every bank-level shock, confirms the composition shift: each
percentage point of CECL exposure moves uninsured time deposits by
0.18 percentage points of assets relative to insured time deposits.
The response is confined to the margin where discipline is feasible:
time deposits reprice only at maturity, and the rollover decision is
the depositor's to make.

The timing of the response separates the two actors in the market.
Banks knew their Day-One charges before depositors could: supervisors
and auditors expected banks to run CECL in parallel with the
incurred-loss model through 2022, and the anticipatory behavior I
document is itself direct evidence that management had a quantified
estimate before the public filing.  Bank-controlled margins move
first.  Insured brokered deposits at high-CECL banks begin
accumulating three quarters before adoption, and the size of that
pre-adoption ramp is predicted by the still-undisclosed future charge;
banks with larger coming charges were also six times more likely to
elect the regulatory capital phase-in, a direct revelation that they
expected a material hit.  Depositor-facing outcomes move only when the
information becomes public: uninsured time deposits are flat through
every pre-adoption quarter and break at disclosure.

The reallocation carries real costs.  High-CECL banks paid
approximately 8--9 basis points more annually in interest expense, ROA
fell by roughly 15 basis points, and NIM compressed by approximately 9
basis points.  Two complementary rate tests show where the cost arises
and where it does not.  Implied rates from Call Report interest
expense show high-CECL banks did not pay more on the uninsured
certificates they retained, and weekly posted deposit rates from
RateWatch show they never raised retail CD or savings rates.  The
funding-cost increase reflects the substitution itself: maturing
uninsured certificates replaced with insured brokered
money raised at wholesale rates, the insured-wholesale channel that
\citet{huberdeau2025adverse} show riskier banks expanded, at higher
rates, through 2023.  Discipline takes the form of a
quantity response: depositors leave rather than negotiate, and the
bank pays for their departure through the higher cost of the insured
wholesale funding that replaces them.

Community banks adopted CECL in the same quarter that the failures of
Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank put bank risk on every
depositor's mind, so I confront the stress confound directly.  Six
pieces of evidence separate the disclosure response from the 2023
stress.  First, the disclosed adjustment is essentially uncorrelated
with the balance-sheet characteristics that governed run exposure in
2023: unrealized securities losses, uninsured deposit shares, and the
other vulnerability measures jointly explain less than one percent of
its cross-sectional variation, and the uninsured share enters with the
wrong sign for a stress story.  Second, the triple-difference
comparison nets out any bank-level exposure to the stress by
construction.  Third, high-CECL banks' posted deposit rates show no
differential movement during the stress weeks themselves.  Fourth, the
bank-side response began in 2022, before the failures.  Fifth, the
March runs themselves were a wholesale phenomenon concentrated at
publicly traded banks, with no retail run and almost no run risk at
private ones \citep{cipriani2024tracing}; the community banks I study
are overwhelmingly private and retail funded.  Sixth, banks that
disclosed a zero Day-One adjustment provide a placebo: among them,
uninsured time deposits did not fall at banks with large unrealized
securities losses after the failures, and adding that loss exposure to
the baseline specification leaves the CECL estimates unchanged.
Separately,
the large SEC-filing banks that adopted CECL in 2020 validate the
signal content of the disclosure: their market capitalization and CDS
spreads moved sharply at adoption in proportion to the charge.  I
exclude that cohort from the deposit tests because it is small
(roughly one hundred publicly traded holding companies) and because
the COVID-19 liquidity programs flooded banks with deposits in
2020, swamping any disciplinary response.

This paper contributes to three literatures.  First, on depositor and
market discipline, prior evidence documents higher rates and larger
outflows at riskier banks \citep{park1995market, park1998market,
egan2017deposit},
but causal interpretation has
been elusive: government guarantees attenuate estimates in the crisis
episodes that generate the most variation \citep{berger2015depositors,
jacewitz2018deposit}, and cross-sectional
comparisons cannot separate information responses from equilibrium
sorting.  Two antecedents come closest, and they define what remains
to be shown.  When regulators were forced to disclose formal
enforcement actions in 1989--1990, thirty-five large publicly traded
holding companies lost roughly 10~percent of large time deposits while
small time deposits rose and total deposits fell less than 2~percent
\citep{jordan1999impact}: the same composition signature, at large
public banks in a crisis.  \citet{chen2022bank} show on Call Report
data that uninsured deposit flows respond more strongly to performance
at more transparent banks, that insured inflows offset the uninsured
outflows, and that the offset is costly.  What neither setting
delivers, and what mandatory CECL adoption does, is predetermined
treatment intensity: a disclosure whose size was fixed by loan
portfolios assembled years earlier, observed at thousands of solvent,
mostly private community banks, at the time-deposit margin where the
rollover decision belongs unambiguously to the depositor.  Work on
the 2023 episode identifies run exposure with the interaction of
mark-to-market losses and uninsured funding \citep{jiang2024monetary};
I exploit variation \emph{within} that high-attention period that is
uncorrelated with those characteristics.  Two further results sharpen
what is at stake.  Retail depositors in a fully uninsured historical
banking system could not tell failing banks from survivors; only
interbank depositors could \citep{blickle2024who}.  And simple
reported profitability predicts crisis tail risk better than
model-based risk measures \citep{meiselman2023judging}.  Whether
ordinary depositors can read and act on a new standardized credit-risk
disclosure is therefore a genuinely open question, and the answer here
is that they can.

Second, on
CECL and mandatory credit-risk disclosure, loan portfolios are the
opaque core of an opaque industry: rating agencies disagree over bank
bonds more than over almost any other sector, and the disagreement
traces to loans and trading assets \citep{morgan2002rating}.
\citet{bushman2012accounting} show that provisioning discretion used
to smooth earnings dampens discipline over bank risk-taking, and
\citet{bushman2015delayed} show that delayed loss recognition degrades
bank transparency and raises downside risk.  The reverse also holds:
measurement rules that force credit risk into reported numbers improve
what outsiders can infer, in the association between bank leverage and
credit risk under fair-value reporting
\citep{blankespoor2013fair} and in the disclosure and
loss-recognition regimes that shaped stakeholder responses during the
2007--2009 crisis \citep{bischof2021accounting}.  Prior CECL work studies the supply side,
lending \citep{granja2025current, chen2022does} and provisioning
\citep{kim2026current}, and the information users it studies are
equity investors \citep{gee2022decision}.  I complement this
literature by tracing the disclosure to depositor behavior and
downstream to funding costs and profitability, a channel the
equity-market strand does not observe.

Third, on deposit
insurance design, explicit insurance weakens the deposit market's
sensitivity to bank risk \citep{demirgucc2004market} and loosens the
constraint it places on bank risk-taking, both in internal loan ratings
\citep{ioannidou2010deposit} and around the 2008 increase in the
coverage limit \citep{lambert2017insured}.  The optimal
coverage limit trades run prevention against the costs of broader
coverage and the mispricing of the guarantee itself
\citep{davila2023optimal, pennacchi2006deposit}.  The uninsured-to-insured
substitution I document has until now been observed at banks in
trouble: failing banks replace fleeing uninsured money with insured
term deposits structured just under the limit \citep{martin2026deposit},
distressed thrifts offset uninsured outflows by attracting insured and
brokered deposits \citep{goldberg2002depositor}, and the 2023 run
banks eventually replaced lost uninsured deposits with higher-cost
insured deposits \citep{cipriani2024tracing}.  In the 2023
cross-section, riskier small and midsize banks expanded reciprocal and
sweep deposits while paying higher rates \citep{huberdeau2025adverse},
and reciprocal-network banks raised insured deposits sharply
\citep{kim2024insurance}.  All of this evidence identifies off
realized distress or contemporaneous balance-sheet risk.  I show the
same repurchasing of insurance as a response to a predetermined
disclosure at solvent banks: much of the bite of depositor discipline
is not lost funding but the price of replacing monitored money with
insured money.
